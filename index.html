Universal RGBW LED controller

A standalone controller that lets Tesla’s interior lighting design studio set colour, brightness and presets on production LED strips without a firmware engineer. Concept to handoff in seven weeks, including my first PCB.

1 Problem and requirements

The interior lighting design studio in LA tests lighting presets for new vehicles. Every time a designer wanted to change a colour or animation, a firmware engineer had to reprogram the LEDs. Each supplier’s LEDs also needed its own proprietary controller, which got expensive as programs scaled. I identified this as a bottleneck and proposed a single standalone controller the designers could run themselves.

ScheduleMid-October start, done by early December 2025. This drove most of the trade-offs below.

2 Prototype 0: proof of concept

Goal. Show that a microcontroller can drive the vehicle’s production LED strip, and work out what a scalable tool would actually need.

Why build P0 from in-house parts only?

Ordering parts costs days. Using what was already in the building (Arduino Uno R3, 16×2 LCD, 4×4 keypad, a potentiometer, a breadboard and a bench 12 V supply) meant I could answer the feasibility question in the first week, before spending anything.

How it worked. The keypad selects a colour channel and triggers “set”, the potentiometer sets that channel from 0 to 255, and the LCD shows prompts and values. The Arduino drives the WS2805 strip over its single-wire data line.

What it taught me. The Uno ran out of pins, firmware updates needed a cable, and adjusting one channel at a time was too slow for a designer. These became the P1 requirements.

Why the ESP32?

It was the only option that met every criterion natively. The deciding factor was over-the-air updates: once the board is sealed in an enclosure in another city, firmware has to be updatable without anyone opening it.

Bring-up. I wired the ESP32 to the sliders, buttons and four strips, adding two 3.3 V to 5 V logic level converters on the LED data lines (reasoning in section 4). Two OLEDs form a dashboard: the left one underlines the selected strip and shows its RGBW values, and the right one shows a 0–100% brightness bar. I simulated the wiring first, then built it.

Terms: buffer = a gate that repeats a signal at a different voltage level. Octal / quad = 8 or 4 buffers in one chip. I²C and UART = the two-wire and serial links the ESP32 uses to talk to the button chip and the display. ADC = the analog input that reads a slider.

Why buffer the LED data lines at all?

The ESP32 drives 3.3 V logic, but the strips’ data inputs expect a high level close to 5 V. A 3.3 V signal sits near the threshold and fails intermittently, especially over long wires. 74HCT and 74AHCT parts, powered at 5 V, have TTL-level inputs (a logic high from about 2 V), so they read 3.3 V cleanly and output a full 5 V swing.

Why 330 Ω in series on every data line?

The data lines run off-board to the strips. A small series resistor near the driver damps ringing and reflections on the cable, and limits current into the first LED’s input if the strip is unpowered or hot-plugged.

Why an I/O expander for the buttons?

P0 ran out of pins. An MCP23017 adds 16 inputs over I²C using just two ESP32 pins, with 4.7 kΩ pull-ups on SDA and SCL. In Rev B the breakout module was in stock, where the bare IC was not.

Why an RC filter on each slider?

The ESP32’s ADC is noisy. A 100 Ω and 100 nF low-pass on each wiper removes high-frequency noise before sampling, so colours don’t flicker when nobody is touching the sliders. Firmware filtering (section 9) handles the rest.

Why move all sliders onto ADC1 in Rev B?

On the ESP32, ADC2 can’t be read while Wi-Fi is running. Wireless updates were a core requirement, so every analog input belongs on ADC1 (GPIO32–39).

Why the bulk and decoupling capacitors?

A 1000 µF electrolytic at the 12 V input absorbs the current steps when strips change brightness quickly, keeping the rail from sagging. A 100 nF ceramic beside every IC (plus 10 µF at the display) handles local high-frequency demand. Pull resistors on the ESP32’s boot-strapping pins make sure it always starts in run mode.

5 Power architecture and fuse sizing

Architecture. A 12 V, 144 W adapter feeds the barrel jack. The 12 V rail goes straight to the LED strips. An LM2596 buck converter supplies 5 V for the display and the level shifters, and the ESP32’s 3.3 V regulator powers the logic (ESP32 and MCP23017). All grounds are common.

Why 1.5× the load?

Fuses are derated for continuous operation. Running one at its full rating for 10+ hours a day causes nuisance blows and heating, so the rated current sits comfortably above the maximum continuous draw.

Why slow-blow?

At power-up the uncharged 1000 µF capacitor looks almost like a short, and the inrush spike can trip a fast-blow fuse even though nothing is wrong. A slow-blow fuse rides through that transient (the capacitor charges in about five time constants) while still opening on a sustained fault.

6 PCB design

A 2-layer mixed-signal board in KiCad, with every component specified and selected by me. I reviewed the layout with the EEs, picked a fab house, ran a DFM check, submitted the PO, then hand-soldered and assembled the boards.

Why 2 layers?

The signals are slow (I²C, UART, LED data around 800 kHz), so two layers with a good ground pour are enough. They’re cheaper and faster to fabricate than four, which mattered on this schedule.

Grounding strategy

Ground pours on both layers, stitched together, give every return current a short, low-impedance path and shield the analog slider traces from the LED data lines. Ground pads connect with thermal reliefs so they can still be hand-soldered.

Placement

Connectors sit on the board edges to match the enclosure openings, sliders and buttons line up with the top-cover slots, and decoupling capacitors sit right beside their IC power pins. High-current 12 V paths to the LED terminals are kept short and wide.

7 Thermal: does the buck converter need a heatsink?

The LM2596 module’s manufacturer notes say no heatsink is needed below 2 A. Our load is far below that, but I wanted to verify the claim myself before sealing the module inside a plastic enclosure.

The model

A steady-state thermal resistance network from the IC to ambient, with an upward convective path and a downward path through the PCB. Each interface is a resistance (for example, convection from the top of the IC: R = 1/(hA) with h = 7 W/m²·K for still air), combined into an equivalent Req ≈ 130 °C/W.

Assumptions, all chosen to be conservative

All heat comes from the one IC (no I²R losses elsewhere), the IC is at a uniform temperature, and radiation is ignored. Each assumption overestimates temperature, so if this passes, the real part passes.

Why SLA and Tough 1500 now?

The first builds will be opened often for testing and fuse changes. SLA gives good dimensional accuracy for snap-fits in a day, and Tough 1500 is formulated for parts that flex repeatedly without cracking.

Why design for moulding from the start?

I used draft angles, uniform wall thickness and ribs instead of thick sections, so the move to injection moulding won’t need a redesign. The rib under the PCB uses the same wall thickness as the outer perimeter to avoid sink marks.

Features and why they’re there

A rib stops the PCB flexing when buttons are pressed (protecting the solder joints). Bosses locate and mount the PCB, and ribs with 1° draft set its height. Walls guide the slider caps, slots align the keycaps, and display mounting bosses set the screen flush with the top face.

Why accept a factor of safety of 1.29?

It’s a prototype, and the method is conservative: it ignores deflection above C, which overestimates stress. The fillet is a stress concentration, so I documented that the geometry gets revised if any yielding appears. Production moves to screws anyway.

9 Firmware

The main loop reads the buttons, reads and filters the sliders, and only pushes a new colour to the strips when something has actually changed.

Debouncing (40 ms)

Mechanical buttons bounce for several milliseconds. Toggling a strip only after the state has held for 40 ms prevents one press from counting several times.

Median-of-3, then an exponential moving average

The median removes single-sample spikes from the noisy ADC, and the EMA (weight 2/10) smooths what’s left while still responding quickly to a real slider move. Readings are mapped from 12-bit to 8-bit and inverted to match the slider direction.

Hysteresis before updating the LEDs

The strips only update when a value changes by more than a threshold. Otherwise the last bit of ADC noise would make colours shimmer and rewrite every pixel for no reason.

Brightness as a scale factor

Brightness multiplies all four channels (bf = Br/255), so dimming keeps the colour’s hue instead of washing it out. Selected strips are written as GRBW frames with NeoPixelBus.

10 Result

The studio has a standalone controller that designers run without engineering support. Each unit drives six times more LEDs than the off-the-shelf controllers it replaced. I handed off the first builds with an SOP and documentation so the team can keep building and maintaining them.

Next steps. Move the enclosure to injection-moulded PC/ABS with screw closure, and bring back Rev A’s full channel count once parts lead time is no longer the constraint.

01 A flex cable kept failing, so I replaced it with a magnet

The need. An accelerometer flex inside the lock kept failing during cycle testing because of its FPCA stack-up. Instead of patching the flex, I asked whether we needed it at all: a magnet on the throw arm and two magnetometers could tell us where the bolt is.

What I did. Designed the experiment: mount the lock with two magnetometers, put a magnet on the throw-arm base, record raw XYZ readings fully retracted and fully thrown, then cycle the bolt while logging. I modified a part so the sense-flex would sit correctly, wired the main board to a PC through a debugger, and worked with firmware on a program that read both sensors at once.

Signal-to-noise ≈ 973

What I had to learn. How to design a clean proof-of-concept experiment, and how to make the case for a design change with data instead of opinion.

Result. The concept held up. Swapping two accelerometers and their FPCAs for magnetometers and insert-moulded magnets cut $12 per unit, about $12M in projected savings.

02 A test fixture that survives the climate chamber

V1. Started from the previous build’s capacitive-touch fixture and redesigned the geometry for the new form factor. Solenoids press the keypad automatically, over and over.

V2. Then it also had to survive environmental testing from −22 °C to 66 °C and physically fit inside the chamber. V2 is smaller and holds the solenoids by their own mounting holes, so the lid is gone, along with the wires that used to catch in the springs.

Result. I designed the fixture and its PCBA, and worked with EE and firmware to resolve 14 issues across 2M+ validation cycles, reaching 100% functional success during EVT.

03 Making the bolt stop binding

The need. The bolt was binding in cycle testing.

What I did. Ran tolerance stack-ups through the mechanism, defined datums to the bolt’s travel path, and tightened the tolerances that actually mattered.

What I had to learn. A lot of GD&T, and how to run a tolerance analysis that points at the real culprit instead of tightening everything.

Result. Actuation-force variability down 25% and mechanism pass rate up 15%, before production.

04 Life testing, DFM and the rest

Cycle testing. Tracked and cycled 12 lock subassemblies for 2.5 months, past 5 million cycles (a factor of safety of 8), and logged every failure.

DFM. Led DFM on 20+ plastic and metal injection-moulded and die-cast parts. In supplier tooling reviews I moved parting lines and gates off cosmetic surfaces, eliminating cosmetic defects and cutting tooling cost 8% without changing the industrial design.

Also. Redesigned a debug-board fixture and made production 2D drawings for magnets, bushings, die-cuts and moulded parts.

What I took away. GD&T, tolerance analysis, DFM, and what you learn from taking apart and reassembling every subassembly in the lock.

01 FrostBot V1 · ME101, Summer 2023

The brief. Automate the icing of circular desserts for consistency, throughput and precision, inside the LEGO EV3 ecosystem with 3D-printed and laser-cut parts, for under $150.

Result. 98% accuracy in icing placement over 100 trials. When the small motors overheated pushing real icing, we switched to Cool Whip and designed a smaller 3D-printed gear for more torque.

02 FrostBot V2 · redesign, October 2025

Why revisit it. V1 worked, but it was flexible, imprecise and its motor overheated on thicker icing. Two years and five co-ops later, I redesigned it as if it were going on a production line.

What changed. An 80/20 aluminium frame for rigidity, a pneumatic piston instead of a motor and worm drive, a motorised z-axis for dessert height, an electric linear slide for radius, a machined Delrin hopper for food contact, and an ESP32 with closed-loop motor control in place of the EV3.

Sizing the piston. I modelled icing as a Bingham plastic, worked back from the flow rate the plate needs to the nozzle pressure and plunger force, then sized the cylinder with a 1.5 factor of safety for standard 90 psi shop air: a 9/16″ bore.

03 Wind-up soccer toy · ME100, Fall 2022

What we built. A portable alternative to foosball for kids aged 6 to 12: a motor turns a cam, the cam drives a follower, and the follower swings an acrylic leg to kick the ball. Laser-cut wood, on a $50 budget per prototype.

How it evolved. The first prototype used a gearbox to drive the kick. The second moved to a TT motor and cam on a car base, which was simpler and kicked more reliably.
